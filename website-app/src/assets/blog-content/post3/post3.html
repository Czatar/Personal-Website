Holy Bingle! I Got Into Google!

Disclaimer: Anything in this blog does not represent Google by any means, it is only sharing my personal experience.

I'm honestly still shocked that this happened. Even if they claim otherwise, every student in computer science wants to work for a MAANG (or whatever the acronym is now) company. Every time someone posts about their success online, the replies get flooded with questions about how they did it, so I wanted to share my personal experience as well. And just to be clear, this was at the L3 level as a new-grad.

As of writing this, I graduated from Texas A&M University (ranked #45 in Computer Science in the US) about 5 months ago. My main selling points were being a peer teacher and interning at LLNL for a year (Over the summer and throughout my senior year). Other than that, my only "personal project" was something I worked on at LLNL which I would consider impressive for an undergraduate student.

The Job Hunt So Far

Given my academics, I felt like I had a solid chance at the job market, but I severely underestimated how competitive 2024 was for recent graduates. In hindsight, my biggest "mistake" was persevering to get a job in Chicago since I was very eager to live in the city. I didn't really care about company prestige, I just wanted the city. I sort of "mass applied" by writing a template cover letter and changing the contents to cater to the specific position I was applying to. I sometimes see candidates make graphs that show what their application distrubtion looked like, so here is mine:

Compared to other candidates, my <100 applications were much less than the 300 to 1000 I've seen. This is most likely due to spending most of my time looking for positions to apply to since I had to specifically filter for Chicago. I sent out my first application on June 6th, 2024, and sent out my last application on July 30th, 2024. This is within 2 months, much less than the typical 4-6 months timeline that I see online. This is mostly due to the fact that I dropped everything (huge risk that paid off) to focus on Google's application (and this website) after they got back to me the first time.

Step 1: Google Reached out to Me First

On July 29th, I received an email from someone on an outreach team encouraging me to apply to their new grad position. I was a bit skeptical about it since it had a broken link in there, but I applied anyways. I didn't think much of it because it's Google, so the odds of this going somewhere seemed slim to me. The next day, I dropped my application process to make my website, which in hindsight was a great use of my time since I would be hearing back from them the day before I made my first blog post public.

Step 2: Snapshot survey and OA

On August 7th, I received an email to get me started with the application process by completing a sort of behavioral survey and online assessment (OA). Since I had been studying leetcode-style questions on CSES, I felt that I would be okay to do the OA that same day, whose due date was a week later. I probably should have spent the week practicing leetcode questions, but I was still skeptical that this would go anywhere, so I just winged it. This was fine since they both felt like medium difficulty questions to me and I finished the OA 30 minutes before the time limit. In hindsight, winging it helped me move along the process quickly because I was emailed by my recruiter the next day saying that my application was reviewed and I would be moving forward in the process.

Step 3: The Onsite interviews

I met with the recruiter through a Google Meet on August 9th to tell me what the process was going to be like. I sent over my availability and the onsites (3 Technical, 1 Googleyness) were all scheduled for Monday, August 26th. Summer was approaching its end, and my brother was taking my car since he was about to start college, so I would be left without a car in Texas. My best friend, who's name is coincidentally also Carlos, offered me to drive up with him to Michigan so that I could use Ann Arbor's Umich campus to focus on applying for jobs (and the Google interviews), to which I agreed. It's his last semester there, so it was smart to take advantage of the study areas. Before leaving, I focused heavily on studying the Neetcode 150. I solved all of the easy problems, then moved on to the mediums, which took a while to get through. After arriving in Michigan on the 22nd, I used the weekend to finish off the medium problems and review hard problems in the areas I was lacking in.

In hindsight, avoiding all of the hard problems was a good idea. I would have spent way too much time scratching my head over them that that it just wasn't worth the time. I didn't solve all of the hards solely because I was running out of time and decided it was a better idea to look up videos of other candidate's experiences with Google onsites and what they think led to their success/failures, which massively helped.

Overall, I got lucky with the entire interview process. What I think helped me succeed was that I talked the entire time. If I got stuck, I didn't show it, but rather I moved on and said "ok let's say for example..." to give myself more thinking time while simultaneously coming up with a test case to work with. I spent more than half of the alloted interview time coming up with the solution before writing any code, so this helped me. I was also sure to ask questions to clarify the input constraints, types, and limitations before moving on to the thought process. I was sure to break down the algorithm into distinct steps and bring up multiple approaches & their time complexities for each step. For one problem, I was following the wrong approach and got stuck during the test case I came up with, and the interviewer gave me a leading question that made it easy to decipher that they wanted to see me use a specific data struture, so I did. Knowing how to interpret hints correctly is a very important interviewing skill that can save you even if you get stuck. Thanks to my extensive C++ practice, I didn't get stuck in the semantics of the language and was able to type out my code quickly, which I think also helped. I peformed very well on my one rescheduled technical on the 29th, so I had time to ask questions. The behavioral interview for "Googleyness" went by smoothly. For this one, I think treating it as a more casual conversation and letting your story flow helps a lot, especially pointing out what you learned from the experience that you're telling.

Step 4: Team Matching

The next day (August 30th) I got a call from the recruiter informing me that everything looked positive on the interviews and asked me to fill out a form with my location/team preferences. Hooray! At this point, I was sure that I had done it. Looking online, I realized that I wasn't out of the water yet and that team matching can be a long process which was a bit nerve wrecking, especially because I had still decided to drop pursuing other jobs until I got a clear answer from Google. However, I improved my odds by specifying that I was flexible on start date, team, and location. Waiting for team matching emails was admittedly stressful.

Thankfully, I had my first team match meeting on September 6th. I did a lot of research into the product area and came up with questions regarding work life, meeting logistics, product technicalities, and what I could do to prepare for starting to work with them. I saw online that some hiring managers would try to ask questions back to test you, but that never happened with me. The second team I met with on the 9th was one that I felt extremely passionate about, so I made sure to refine my questions as much as I could. Miraculously, I was told on the 10th that I was their top candidate for the team and would be confirmed with them. I genuinely couldn't believe that I had made it this far so quickly considering team matching was a notoriously long process for many. I had a phone call with my recruiter regarding compensation, so it sounded like there was little to nothing that would cause me to lose my chances with Google.

Step 5: Waiting for the Offer

Due to some challenges, the team no longer needed the position, as I was told on September 26th. On September 27th, I was sent another team matching meeting, which instilled hope in me again. I flew back to Texas that Sunday to be ready in case I had to move (in hindsight, this was another risk). On September 30th, I had the meeting, and it went really well! It was strucutred the same as the first two meetings I had, so I asked the same questions that were previously met with positive reception. Being socially adept is a very good skill to have for these meetings. The next day, October 1st, I was confirmed for that team, and on October 2nd, I was officially sent the offer letter and of course, I signed it!

Final Thoughts

I think it was a 25% Skill and 75% Luck distribution that contributed to my success. How they found me and encouraged me to apply in the first place is beyond me. In the beginning, I said that it was a "mistake" to limit myself to Chicago, but this turned out to be a blessing in disguise, because if I went for something easier, then there's a chance that I would have been locked into a job before Google could get the chance to reach out to me. What's really funny is that it was only 3 weeks after arriving at Michigan that I was told about getting the offer, so I spent way less time here than I origininally thought.

Another stroke of luck was that I got a very good recruiter, and I could tell that they were doing everything in their power to make the process as smooth as possible for me.

I am still shocked that despite what everyone has been saying about the CS job market in 2024, I landed the offer with Google and made it that far in the first place! This is absolutely not something I will take for granted. Think of how many skilled candidates from the top universities there are, and they chose me? I also think my interest in the team that chose me was a very niche area since it doesn't involve web development or AI in any regard, which is easily more than (probably) two thirds of what CS students want to do.

Another aspect to think about is that I never performed that well on Codeforces. My elo only went up to 1300 at most, which is considered the middle of the Pupil candidate. And right now I'm back to newbie rank, so I never thought that I was amazing at competitive programming. It could be that my rank is still better than the vast majority of candidates and those better than me on competitive programming platforms are just the top .1% of candidates that exist, and competing against them would be rare in the first place. Who knows!

Lastly, I think the timing for when I will start working for Google is insanely ideal. My first student loan payment is due starting on January 2025, so by then I'll have a good bonus that will cover those costs without a hitch. It's still (understandably) upsetting to most that Google's application process takes so long. My case is considered lucky, and it was still a grand total of 2 entire months between sending my first applicaiton to landing an offer, so I can see why most would be devastated if they had to endure that long just for a low chance at landing something, so the gravity of my situation is still astounding to me. To all attempting to land a job at Google, good luck!
